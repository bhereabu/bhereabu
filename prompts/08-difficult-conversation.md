# 8. Practice a difficult conversation

Most people walk into a hard conversation knowing what they want to say, and not
what the other person will say back. That's the half that goes wrong.

Claude plays the other person: responds the way they actually would, pushes back
when your argument is weak, and makes you earn the outcome. After two or three
run-throughs the real conversation is noticeably easier.

## Paste at the start of a new chat

```
I need to prepare for a difficult conversation. I want you to roleplay as the other person so I can practice.

The person: [describe who they are - boss, client, co-founder, etc.]
What I need to say: [what you need to ask or tell them]
Why it's hard: [what you're afraid of / what could go wrong]
What this person is like: [their personality, how they typically react, what they care about]

Stay in character throughout the conversation. Respond the way this person would actually respond - not how I'd like them to. If I say something weak or unconvincing, push back on it.

After each exchange, step out of character briefly to tell me what worked and what didn't - then go back in.

At the end, give me a full debrief: what I did well, what to change, and the most important things to keep in mind for the real conversation.
```

## Getting a realistic counterpart

The quality of the roleplay is set entirely by the "what this person is like"
field. Vague input ("kind of difficult") produces a generic obstacle. Specifics
produce the actual person:

- What they optimize for (budget, headcount, being seen as decisive, avoiding risk)
- How they react under pressure (goes quiet, gets tactical, redirects to process)
- What they've said before in similar conversations, quoted if you remember it
- What they know that you don't

## Run it more than once

The first run is you finding out where your argument is soft. The value is in
runs two and three, after you've fixed it. Worth trying at least once with an
explicit difficulty bump:

```
Run it again. This time they're in a worse mood and start by saying no.
```

## Caveat

You're rehearsing against a model of the person, not the person. It'll surface
the objections you should have prepared for; it can't tell you what they'll
actually decide. Prepare for the pushback, stay flexible about the outcome.
